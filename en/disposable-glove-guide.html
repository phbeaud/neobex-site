Disposable Glove Guide

Disposable gloves are the item most businesses buy most often, and the one they compare least. Two boxes that look alike can differ in price by a factor of two, and two gloves at the same price can have completely different lifespans in real use.

This guide gathers what our clients ask us most: how to read a technical data sheet, what the numbers on the box actually mean, and how to avoid paying for a feature that does nothing in your workplace. It is written for purchasers, not for specialists.

1. The four materials and their uses

Almost every disposable glove on the market is made of one of these four materials. The material determines strength, comfort, chemical compatibility and price.

Nitrile

Nitrile is now the default choice in most workplaces. It is a synthetic rubber with no latex proteins, so there is no risk of latex allergy. It resists tears, punctures, oils and greases well, and it tears visibly when it fails — an advantage in food and healthcare settings, where you want to see immediately that a glove needs changing.

Vinyl (PVC)

Vinyl is the economical option. It suits short, repetitive, low-risk tasks: food service, prep work, light cleaning, handling with no chemical contact. It is less elastic and punctures more easily. In prolonged use, the savings at purchase are often lost to higher consumption.

Latex

Natural latex offers the best dexterity and fit: it is the glove you forget you are wearing. Its drawback is well known — part of the population develops an allergy to latex proteins, and that allergy can affect staff and customers alike. Many businesses have removed it entirely for that reason alone.

Polyethylene (PE)

The polyethylene glove, often called a "poly" or food-service glove, is the cheapest of all. It is loose-fitting and only suits very brief contact: a service counter, a one-off handling task, a glove change for each customer. It is not protective equipment.

Key point

No disposable glove protects against every chemical. Compatibility depends on the specific product, its concentration and the contact time. If your staff handle chemicals, ask the manufacturer for permeation data on that specific product, not a general claim.

2. Thickness: understanding the mil

Glove thickness is expressed in mils: one thousandth of an inch, or 0.0254 mm. The measurement is usually taken at the fingertip, where the glove is thickest; the palm and cuff are thinner. That is why two gloves both labelled "4 mil" may not behave the same — you need to know where the measurement was taken.

Some suppliers give the glove weight in grams instead. That is a more honest basis for comparison, because it accounts for the whole glove. At equal size, a heavier glove contains more material.

Common thicknesses and matching uses
ThicknessTypeTypical use
2 to 3 milVery thinBrief contact, food service, very frequent changes
3 to 5 milStandard examinationHealthcare, laboratory, food processing, general cleaning
5 to 8 milReinforcedProlonged wear, light mechanical work, cleaning chemicals, handling
8 mil and upIndustrialShop work, sharp parts, solvents, long dirty jobs

The trade-off never changes: the thicker the glove, the longer it lasts, but the less precise the hand. In detailed work, a glove that is too thick wastes time and gets removed — which cancels the protection entirely. In dirty work, a glove that is too thin gets changed five times as often, and the real cost climbs.

3. Sizing and palm width

Disposable glove size is set by palm width, measured flat, excluding the thumb, across the knuckles. The values below are those generally used in the industry; each manufacturer has its own tolerances, and the data sheet remains the reference.

Approximate size correspondence
SizePalm widthReference point
XSabout 70 mmSlim hands
Sabout 80 mmMost requested size in healthcare
Mabout 95 mmMost common size across all industries
Labout 110 mmSecond most ordered size
XLover 110 mmIndustrial settings, warehouses

A sizing error is paid for in consumption. A glove that is too large bunches at the fingertips, catches and punctures; one that is too small pulls across the palm, tires the hand and tears at the cuff while being put on. In a team, the real size distribution is never uniform: ordering cases of medium only guarantees waste at both ends.

Practical tip

Before an annual order, take a quick inventory of the sizes actually worn at each workstation. It is the single exercise that reduces consumption the most, before you even change product.

4. Texture, cuff, powder and colour

Texture

A smooth glove gives the best touch. A glove textured at the fingertips or over the whole hand grips better, especially around water or grease. In a kitchen, a shop or a dairy, texture is not a cosmetic detail: it is what keeps you from dropping what you are holding.

Cuff length

A standard cuff stops at the wrist. Long cuffs, 12 inches and up, protect the forearm during cleaning, decanting or liquid handling. A beaded cuff stays in place better and keeps liquids from running into the glove.

Powder

Powdered gloves were easier to don, but the powder carried latex proteins into the air and left residue on surfaces and food. Powdered medical gloves have been banned from sale in the United States since 2017 and have become very rare in Canada. Powder-free is the standard.

Colour

Colour serves a real purpose. Blue and purple are the standards in healthcare and laboratories. Black is common in mechanical and beauty work, where it hides dirt. Bright blue is preferred in food processing: a fragment of glove that falls into a product is spotted immediately, because no food is that colour. Some businesses also use two colours to separate work zones and avoid cross-contamination.

6. Standards to require from a supplier

This is the section most purchasers skip, and the one that separates a real supplier from an opportunistic reseller. A serious supplier sends these documents without hesitation and without delay.

Warning sign

A supplier who cannot name the factory that makes the glove, or provide the data sheet and lot number, is selling a product whose consistency they do not control. This is common with very cheap imported gloves: the first shipment is compliant, the next ones are not, and you only see the difference once the case is open.

7. Calculating order quantities

A glove's unit price depends almost entirely on order volume. To get a real price, you need to know your consumption. The calculation fits on one line:

People wearing gloves × pairs per day × working days in the period = pairs needed.

A box of 100 gloves provides about 50 pairs. Divide the pairs needed by 50 to get the number of boxes.

Example: 12 people changing gloves 6 times a day, 250 days a year, comes to 18,000 pairs — about 360 boxes of 100 gloves per year. At that volume you no longer order by the box but by the case or the pallet, and the unit price moves into a different bracket.

Two adjustments to plan for: the size distribution, which is never uniform, and warehouse space. A pallet price is worthless if the pallet does not fit in your building. In that case, a price negotiated on annual volume with staggered deliveries gives the same result without tying up your space.

8. The mistakes that cost the most

  • Comparing the price of the box instead of the price per glove used. A cheaper box where staff use two gloves instead of one costs more by month-end. The right unit of comparison is cost per person per day.
  • Ordering a single size. This is the most frequent cause of overconsumption and of gloves torn while being put on.
  • Going thicker "to be safe". In precision work, an overly thick glove ends up removed, and then there is no protection at all.
  • Switching supplier without samples. A data sheet does not tell you how a glove behaves in your staff's hands at your workstations. A sample tested for a week on the real job settles it.
  • Accepting a price without validating lot consistency. A good price on a single lot is not a good supply price.
